1. Executive judgement

Purpose

This is a generic comparative reference. It describes how secondary-care integration is organised across the four UK nations and how those architectures relate to emerging EHDS concepts. It does not assume a single UK-wide system, recommend a particular product architecture or assess any specific implementation.

Analysis

There is no single UK secondary-care integration architecture. The four nations share standards and many clinical concepts, but expose materially different integration surfaces. England is predominantly a set of explicit national APIs and services; Wales is closer to a nationally integrated platform and shared-repository model; Scotland combines established national and Board services with a transition towards the National Digital Platform; Northern Ireland has consolidated secondary and community care around encompass/Epic, with primary care remaining outside that boundary.

This means meaningful comparison should be made capability by capability, asking where information originates, which national or regional service mediates it, whether the flow is read, write or bidirectional, and whether the route is a public API, national message service, shared repository, portal or local integration.

Assurance

Amber

The comparison is sufficiently evidenced to support architecture discovery and informed comparison. It is not a substitute for nation-specific onboarding, contractual confirmation or local integration discovery.

Particular caution is required for Northern Ireland external APIs and for Scottish services still moving towards target-state NDP patterns.

2. The four national integration patterns

European comparison

Alignment with the emerging European Health Data Space architecture

EHDS provides a useful external reference architecture even though the four UK health services are not EU Member State implementations. This comparison assesses structural and technical alignment, not legal compliance or certification.

Legal and architectural context

Fact: Regulation (EU) 2025/327 creates a common architecture for primary use, secondary use and EHR-system interoperability. The first primary-use priority group is Patient Summary plus ePrescription/eDispensation from March 2029; imaging, medical test results and discharge reports follow from March 2031. The detailed European electronic health record exchange format (EEHRxF) and several other implementing specifications are due by 26 March 2027. WEB-19 · WEB-20

Analysis: Because those detailed implementing specifications are still emerging, a UK system cannot meaningfully claim full EHDS conformance in September 2026. The useful question is whether national architecture would make future mapping to EHDS comparatively straightforward.

Scope caution

Fact: MyHealth@EU connects EU national infrastructures through National Contact Points for eHealth, while HealthData@EU provides the federated secondary-use model. None of the four UK nations currently operates as an EU Member State node in those infrastructures. WEB-21 · WEB-23

Legal/professional inference: Northern Ireland's Windsor Framework position should not be treated as making EHDS automatically applicable. New EU acts require the specific Windsor Framework process before they apply in Northern Ireland. WEB-28

WEB-28
Architecture and interoperability implications

Implications for architecture and interoperability practice

  1. Keep the six EHDS priority categories explicit in the information architecture. Patient Summary, prescriptions/dispensations, imaging, tests/results and discharge reports should each have governed import/export contracts rather than being hidden inside generic document exchange.
  2. Keep national integration boundaries explicit. National APIs, repositories, gateways and messaging services should be treated as distinct architectural boundaries so that future UK-to-EU exchange can be added without distorting domestic clinical architectures.
  3. Preserve provenance, terminology and source identifiers. This will matter when mapping UK Core, national profiles and terminology into the final EEHRxF specifications.
  4. Make audit data exportable. EHDS treats logging as a harmonised EHR component, so audit should not be an opaque vendor-only subsystem.
  5. Treat portable structured export as an architectural requirement. Even before EEHRxF is final, systems should support loss-aware export and re-import of priority clinical information using open, documented formats.
  6. Keep secondary-use architecture separable from operational clinical-system storage. Data catalogue, access approval, secure processing and disclosure controls should be capable of mapping to an HDAB/HealthData@EU-style operating model if cross-border participation later becomes desirable.

Assessment basis: qualitative architectural alignment as at 13 September 2026. Green means structurally close to the emerging EHDS model; Amber means materially aligned but incomplete or transitional; Amber–Red means a significant architectural or governance gap. These are not legal compliance ratings.

5. Cross-border implications

Fact

Identity is nationally scoped

England and Wales are NHS-number centred, Scotland uses CHI, and Northern Ireland uses the Health and Care Number supported by NIDIS. Cross-border and multi-jurisdiction architectures should therefore preserve assigning authority and identifier provenance rather than assume one universal person identifier.

Analysis

The common layer should be semantic, not infrastructural

The viable common denominator is a stable semantic and provenance discipline. The different API, messaging, repository and portal patterns should be mediated explicitly rather than forced into one canonical transport architecture.

Unresolved

External access to encompass needs direct confirmation

Public DHCNI material clearly describes EpicCare Link, but does not publish a public structured integration catalogue comparable with NHS England or DHCW. Any cross-organisational integration initiative should treat the available machine-to-machine route as an early discovery and contractual question.

Practical cross-border flow

Patient presents in another UK nation → local national identity is resolved → available source information is retrieved through that nation's services → provenance and original coding are retained → secondary care records the new episode → discharge/transfer information is returned through the receiving nation's available route.

That is a mediated transfer of information, not a single record moving transparently between four health services.

7. Limitations

Unresolved: Public documentation is uneven. England and Wales publish relatively detailed API and integration material; Scotland publishes more target-state programme information; Northern Ireland's public material is stronger on encompass service boundaries than on external machine-to-machine interfaces. Absence of a published API is therefore not evidence that no interface exists.

Scope: This resource compares the integration surface around secondary care across the four UK nations. It does not attempt to catalogue every local Trust, Board or specialty interface, nor does it constitute clinical-safety, cyber-security, information-governance or regulatory assurance.

Currency: National digital programmes change. The resource is dated 13 September 2026 and should be versioned when material national services or onboarding routes change.
