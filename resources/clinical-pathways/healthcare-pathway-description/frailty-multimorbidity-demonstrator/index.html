What if a clinical pathway meant the same thing wherever it was used?

This demonstrator shows why a standard way of describing pathways could help clinical teams coordinate care across organisations without forcing everyone to use the same system.

The problem is not that clinicians lack pathways

The problem is that pathway meaning is often scattered across documents, local procedures, EPR configuration, referral forms and tacit knowledge. That makes it hard to compare pathways, change systems safely, or coordinate care across organisational boundaries.

1

Different teams describe the same pathway differently

Important details such as who coordinates care, what information is needed and when escalation occurs may be expressed inconsistently.

2

The pathway becomes tied to today's systems

If pathway meaning is embedded in an EPR or local workflow, changing technology can accidentally change the care model.

3

Cross-organisational care is difficult to see as one whole

Frailty care can cross general practice, community services, social care, pharmacy, urgent response, virtual wards and hospitals.

The proposed answer: describe the pathway once as a governed clinical and business artefact, then link local systems and organisational arrangements to it separately.

Why use a standard at all?

A standard is valuable only if it improves clinical understanding, coordination and safe change. The purpose is not to create more documentation.

Without a common representation

  • Pathways are hard to compare across services.
  • Responsibilities may be implicit.
  • Important information requirements are buried in prose.
  • Local system design can become confused with clinical intent.
  • Changing an EPR may require rediscovering how the pathway actually works.
  • Evidence and provenance may be disconnected from the pathway.

With a common representation

  • Every pathway has a stable identity and common set of fields.
  • Population, purpose, activities, decisions, roles and outcomes are explicit.
  • Information needs are linked to the pathway rather than to a particular system.
  • Local technology sits in an implementation layer.
  • Pathways can be compared, related and versioned.
  • Evidence and governance remain traceable.

What this should mean for clinical staff

Less ambiguity

It should be easier to see what the pathway is actually asking teams to do.

Safer change

Changing systems need not silently redefine the model of care.

Better hand-offs

Cross-organisational information and responsibilities can be made explicit.

The shared record is important, but it is not the pathway

The pathway tells us what coordinated care requires. A Single Patient Record or shared care record provides some of the information needed to support that care.

Evidence, confidence and local validation

A pathway can be well supported by guidance and still not be locally approved. The demonstrator keeps evidence provenance separate from clinical authority.

Why that distinction matters

An evidence label such as Directly grounded in guidance tells you where the reference content came from. It does not mean a local clinical governance body has approved the pathway or that a local service actually works this way.

How to use this demonstrator
Designed for clinical and service staff first
Start with the guided tour
The first five steps use a familiar frailty scenario to explain the value of a standard without requiring knowledge of modelling languages, FHIR, BPMN or software architecture.
Then explore a pathway you recognise
The pathway page is deliberately written in clinical language: who is this for, what starts it, what are we trying to achieve, who is involved, what decisions matter, what information needs to travel and what happens next.
Operability
Operability means that the pathway remains understandable, usable, coordinated, monitorable and safely adaptable across teams, organisations, settings, systems and time. Technical interoperability is one part of that wider problem.
What is optional
The technical section contains HPDS mappings, conformance levels and JSON. Clinical readers do not need to understand these to assess whether the proposed standard adds value.
