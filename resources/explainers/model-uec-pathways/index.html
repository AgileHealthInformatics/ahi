What the models are

NHS England published the Model ED and the Model Acute Pathway on 9 February 2026, and the Model discharge pathway on 7 July 2026. Read separately, each looks like a set of local expectations for one part of the hospital. Read together, they describe a single chain of events from arrival to leaving, each with an expected time and a named owner.

The Model Emergency Department sets out how an ED should be organised internally, from the front door to the point at which a patient leaves. The Model Acute Pathway, developed with the Royal College of Physicians, the Society for Acute Medicine and the British Geriatrics Society, sets fifteen standards for the first 72 hours of hospital care for acutely unwell patients. A self-assessment tool accompanies the acute standards and describes how trusts should evidence and report their position to the board.

The Model discharge pathway applies to acute and community bedded care, for patients of all ages and for both physical and mental health. It covers in-hospital discharge planning and delivery, the daily ward practices within the control of hospital teams, and the standardisation of basic processes. It refers back to the other two models directly, describing a whole pathway approach in which early senior decision-making in the first 72 hours makes timely discharge possible later.

This explainer treats the three documents as one system: arrival, assessment, decision, referral, review, request, transfer, readiness and discharge. It draws on a conceptual information model and business capability model built from the standards to show the structure that the text implies.

PRN02373

The Model Emergency Department

Internal ED pathways: streaming and redirection, rapid assessment and treatment, resus, extended emergency medicine ambulatory care (EEMAC), priority cohorts, and enablers such as coding, site-level data and payment.

PRN02076

The Model Acute Pathway

Fifteen standards for acute receiving areas in the first 72 hours, grouped under early decision-making, care navigation, continuity, 7-day working, data recording, patients and carers, and board assurance.

Companion guidance

Self-assessment tool

Twelve insightful questions, four national measures, suggested evidence for each standard and an outline board assurance report, completed at baseline and then at least every six months.

PRN02543

The Model discharge pathway

Seven principles of discharge, core elements for acute and community hospitals, the organisational arrangements needed to deliver them, discharge pathway definitions 0 to 3, and cohorts at greater risk from delayed discharge.

What they aim to achieve

The Model ED aims to make the emergency department a destination rather than a default. The Model Acute Pathway aims to make the first 72 hours after admission timely, continuous and clearly owned. The Model discharge pathway aims to make timely, clinically-led discharge routine, so that patients leave hospital as soon as they no longer need to be there.

The Model ED starts from five principles: the ED is for patients most in need of emergency care; overcrowding makes it harder to find the sickest and most vulnerable patients; overcrowding also makes the whole pathway less efficient; patients should not come to harm through undue waits; and the ED cannot deliver good care without the rest of the hospital. It supports the constitutional pledge that 95% of A&E patients are admitted, transferred or discharged within four hours.

The Model Acute Pathway begins with the scale of the problem it addresses: 1.7 million people spent 12 hours or more in EDs in 2024, and average length of stay is around 10% longer than before the pandemic. Its five themes are early senior decision-making with patient and carer involvement, continuity and coordination, care in the right place at the right time, 7-day working, and accurate capture of decisions and activity. If fully implemented, it expects shorter ED stays, fewer 12-hour waits and less corridor care, better quality and safety, less distress for carers, clearer ownership of care, and fewer unnecessary long stays.

The Model discharge pathway rests on seven principles: discharge is a clinical decision, not an administrative one, planned from admission; staying in hospital longer than necessary causes harm; timely discharge is essential to flow and safety across the urgent and emergency pathway; home is the default destination; discharge is everyone's business every day, with clear decision rights; patients and carers are partners; and decisions rest on timely, transparent data. It asks for a change of mindset in which only patients who need inpatient care are admitted and discharge is treated as part of care from the point of admission.

Where the documents meet. The Model ED governs care until the ED has finished; the acute standards take over on the acute receiving area. The discharge model does not simply follow the acute pathway. It overlaps it, because discharge planning starts at admission and the same 6 and 14 hour expert review is the point at which both models expect the plan to be set. The boundaries between the three documents are where most of the practical difficulty in winter sits.

The three clocks

All three documents express many of their expectations as elapsed time between two recorded events. That makes them measurable, and also makes them dependent on data.

Lens

    The model underneath

    A conceptual information model names the things an organisation must be able to record and says how they relate. It is not a system design and does not prescribe a product.

    Its value here is that every time limit in the standards depends on two records existing, being timed, and being linked to the same patient and the same episode. Where one of the pair is missing, the standard cannot be evidenced, however well the care was delivered. The model built from the two documents gathers around a small number of ideas. Each group below is described in plain terms, with the model's own entity names shown for those who will work with the repository.

    Proposed extension for discharge

    The current model records the end of an encounter or location stay, but holds no discharge records of its own. The Model discharge pathway depends on several things that need to exist as records before its expectations can be evidenced. The entities below are proposed to close that gap. They are shown dashed throughout this page and are not yet in the model repository.

    DischargePlan
    Reason for admission, expected date of discharge, intended pathway and destination, set from admission and revised daily.
    DischargeCriterion
    Patient-specific physiological and functional criteria agreed by an expert decision-maker, against which nurses and therapists can discharge.
    FunctionalBaseline
    The patient's usual abilities, recorded on admission so that recovery goals and deconditioning can be judged.
    CriteriaToResideAssessment
    The daily judgement of whether the patient still needs care that only an inpatient setting can give.
    DischargeReadiness DischargePathwayAssignment
    The discharge ready date, when criteria to reside are no longer met, and the assigned pathway from 0 to 3.
    DischargeTask
    Preparatory work completed in parallel: medicines to take out, therapy, equipment, documentation and transport.
    Discharge TransferOfCare
    The discharge event, with time, pathway and destination, and the handover of clinical responsibility to a community service.
    DischargeDelay
    Reason, named owner, actions and timescales for each patient not discharged as expected.
    CareTransferHubReferral CommunityCapacity
    Referral of complex discharges to the care transfer hub, and the community capacity that discharge teams need to see.
    PostDischargeFollowUp
    A follow-up contact after discharge, such as a telephone call within 24 hours for older people with frailty.

    The same approach would add capabilities beneath Flow Management and Escalation for criteria-led discharge, discharge delay management, transfer of care co-ordination and discharge lounge operations, each traced to these records.

    Limitations

    The models are a sound basis for planning and assurance. They also have boundaries that leaders should keep in view when they use them.

    Capacity

    Standards set expectations; they do not create capacity

    The 12-hour transfer standard and the 72-hour limit on acute receiving areas depend on downstream beds, discharge and social care. Daily specialty review while a patient waits manages risk during a delay without removing its cause.

    Scope

    Deliberately bounded scope

    The Model ED addresses elements within the ED's control and states that further guidance will follow on urgent community services, 7-day working, mental health EDs, neighbourhood health, frailty and children's pathways. The Model discharge pathway refers to a Community Beds Delivery Model and Discharge Ready Date guidance that were pending when it was published, and separate guidance covers discharge from mental health inpatient settings.

    Measurement

    ED time sits outside the 72 hours

    The acute standards do not yet count time spent in the ED within the first 72 hours, and ask teams to take a patient-oriented approach. A long ED wait can consume much of the window in which early expert review matters, without appearing in the acute measures.

    Comparison

    Local determination limits comparison

    Medical and nursing directors decide locally how to deliver the standards. Self-assessments and board reports are not collected nationally, and only a small number of national benchmarking measures are planned.

    Evidence

    Much is evidenced by policy, not data

    Seven of the fifteen acute standards have a suggested data report; the other eight are evidenced by written policies, rosters, job plans and audits. The discharge model has no self-assessment tool. It asks for a baseline against its core elements and two operational metrics, with a review of discharge data requirements to follow and a refreshed sitrep as a time-limited measure.

    Data

    Data readiness is uneven

    The self-assessment tool acknowledges that treatment function coding and some timestamps may not yet be recorded, and asks trusts to run the measures anyway and surface the gaps. A national review of ED counting and coding began in 2026, with revised guidance expected later in the year.

    Quality

    Time limits measure timeliness only

    A clock met shows that a decision-maker of the right tier was present in time. It does not show that the decision was right. Standard 14, on dignity, involvement and information for patients and carers, has no time limit and relies on feedback and notes audit.

    Interpretation

    The conceptual model is an interpretation

    The information and capability models are a reading of the published text, structured to make dependencies visible. They do not describe staffing, demand or capacity, and each trust's systems will hold these records differently. The acute standards are framed for adults; paediatric care is guided by Facing the Future. The discharge records shown dashed are a proposed extension, not yet in the repository.

    Partners

    Discharge depends on partners outside the hospital

    The discharge model sets no new requirements for social care providers. Pathways 1 to 3 depend on local authorities, social care, housing and voluntary sector partners, through commissioned arrangements and the Better Care Fund. Hospital records can show where a delay sits; they cannot by themselves resolve it.

    Winter 2026/27

    NHS England's winter planning letter for 2026/27 (PRN02603) asks trusts to implement the Model ED, Model acute pathway and Model discharge pathway requirements before winter, and treats the elimination of corridor care as a patient safety priority. All three models covered here are therefore part of this year's winter expectations.

    Implementation

    Requirements in place before winter

    Model ED, Model acute pathway and Model discharge pathway requirements to be implemented ahead of winter.

    Corridor care

    A patient safety priority

    Elimination of corridor care is treated as a patient safety priority. The acute pathway board report counts patients cared for in corridors.

    Ambulance handover

    15 minutes, never over 45

    Ambulance services to work with acute partners towards 15-minute handovers, with none exceeding the 45-minute maximum.

    System escalation

    OPEL coordination

    ICBs to run a 7-day coordination centre using the OPEL framework, with tested escalation, surge and mutual aid arrangements.

    Board assurance

    Statements signed by 30 September

    Signed board assurance statements were due by 30 September 2026, following regional stress tests in September.

    Acute pathway reporting

    A six-monthly cycle

    Baseline self-assessment formed part of winter 2025/26 assurance, with progress reports to the board at least every six months.

    Discharge with partners

    Occupancy down before Christmas

    ICBs to commission social care and intermediate care jointly with local authorities, with discharge planned together, sufficient capacity through winter and agreed plans to reduce hospital occupancy ahead of Christmas.

    Surge planning

    Contingency agreed in advance

    The discharge model asks for surge and contingency plans with system partners, including funding, for periods when demand exceeds planned provision, particularly in winter.

    Where winter shows in the architecture

    Pressure moves backwards through the chain, and often starts with a patient who is ready to leave but has not. A patient waiting for a downstream bed holds an acute receiving bed, which holds an ED cubicle, which delays an ambulance handover. Each link is an interval between two records in the model, so each can be watched separately and the point of first failure identified.

    Using the models over the coming months

    1. Check that each clock has both of its records. For every time limit above, confirm that the start and end events are captured with times and linked to the same episode. Where they are not, record the gap in the board report as the self-assessment tool advises.
    2. Make the boundary explicit. Agree in the internal professional standards who is responsible for a patient referred from the ED who has no acute receiving bed, and how disputes about responsibility or location are escalated.
    3. Hold the daily rhythm over holidays. Confirm that board rounds, consultant-led ward rounds, huddles and senior-led handovers are rostered on every weekend and bank holiday day, as Standards 7 and 8 require.
    4. Watch every link in the chain. Report the intervals above alongside 4-hour and 12-hour performance, preferably as SPC charts, so that the first link to slow down is visible before it reaches the front door.
    5. Make discharge visible every day. Report discharges required and delivered by pathway, and patients with no criteria to reside, at ward and site level, as the discharge model asks. Where these cannot yet be recorded, the proposed discharge records show what would be needed.
    6. Feed the six-monthly report. Use winter experience in the next self-assessment, against the twelve insightful questions and the outline board assurance report.
