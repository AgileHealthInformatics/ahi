1. Executive judgement

Healthcare does not need one information model. It needs a governed chain of models whose purposes are explicit.

The strongest architecture separates conceptual meaning, reusable clinical content, use-case constraints, exchange representations, persistence and secondary-use models. Translation between these layers is expected. The risk arises when a transformation is treated as semantically neutral or when one successful model is promoted beyond the purpose for which it was designed.

Green: purpose-specific modellingAmber: model translation requires governanceAmber–Red: universal canonicalisation

Model the meaning before the message

Information requirements should exist independently of the exchange technology. A FHIR profile can express a computable implementation contract, but it should not be the only place where the clinical requirement is defined.

Let models specialise

openEHR archetypes, CDA documents, FHIR resources, DICOM objects, OMOP tables and CDISC study models make different trade-offs because they serve different purposes. That is a strength, not evidence of standards failure.

Govern every translation

Mapping from a source record into a profile, common data model, classification or analytical mart can alter meaning. Preserve the source semantics, mapping rationale, terminology versions and provenance.

The navigator distinguishes models by purpose, abstraction and lifecycle position.

3. Model anatomy

Five modelling layers that should remain distinguishable.

Real implementations may combine them, but governance becomes clearer when each layer has a named purpose and owner.

Concept & metadata layer

Defines the care concepts, data-element meanings, relationships, terminology bindings and enterprise definitions that should survive individual implementations. Examples: ISO 13940, ISO/IEC 11179, terminology concept models.

Clinical content & record layer

Structures clinically coherent information for capture and longitudinal use. Examples: PRSB information models, openEHR archetypes/templates, ISO 13606 archetypes.

Exchange & document layer

Represents information for a particular interoperability contract or persistent artefact. Examples: FHIR resources/profiles/logical models, CDA documents and DICOM information objects.

Analytical & research layer

Normalises or reshapes source information for reporting, cohort discovery, observational research or clinical trials. Examples: OMOP, i2b2, CDISC ODM/SDTM and dimensional models.

Preservation & traceability layer

Retains the source models, versions, mappings, terminology context and representation information needed to understand records after systems and models change.

4. Decision trail

Information modelling evolved by solving different constraints rather than converging on one universal representation.

Clinical documents preserved authored context; archetype approaches separated stable technical models from evolving domain content; FHIR made structured information accessible through mainstream developer tooling; common data models normalised heterogeneous data for reproducible analysis. Each step addressed a different problem.

5. Standards and policy basis

The key standards occupy different abstraction levels.

A national architecture can legitimately use ISO 13940 for concepts, ISO/IEC 11179 for governed data definitions, PRSB-style content requirements, FHIR for exchange, openEHR for longitudinal persistence, DICOM for imaging and OMOP for research. The governance challenge is the mapping, not the coexistence.

8. Decision aid

Build a model stack around the problem rather than declaring a universal canonical model.

The recommendations deliberately combine models at different layers. The distinction between primary and supporting models matters more than selecting a single winner.

Six questions before modelling
1Is the purpose clinical capture, exchange, persistence, analytics, research or preservation?
2Which concepts and data-element meanings must remain stable across implementations?
3Does the model need to represent a persistent authored artefact, a mutable clinical state or an analytical derivative?
4Which terminology and value-set constraints carry clinical meaning?
5What will be lost when source information is transformed into this model?
6Can a future user reconstruct the source model, version, mapping and provenance?
9. Translation and viability

The highest-risk point is often between models.

These transitions are normal architectural work. The assurance requirement is to make the semantic change explicit.

Concept → clinical content

Requirements become structures

Check that every important business/clinical concept remains traceable into the content model rather than being inferred from field names.

Content → FHIR profile

Information becomes an exchange contract

Record cardinality changes, extensions, terminology bindings and elements that cannot be represented without approximation.

Clinical record → document

Mutable state becomes a snapshot

Preserve authorship, effective time and narrative context; do not treat the document as automatic current truth.

Clinical record → OMOP/i2b2

Care data becomes research data

Record source-to-standard concepts, derived dates and loss of workflow or uncertainty context.

EHR → archive

Application semantics must outlive software

Retain source model definitions, terminology versions, reference tables and provenance alongside archived records.

Local model → canonical model

Mediation can simplify or flatten

Make the canonical model's scope explicit and retain reversibility where clinically significant distinctions would otherwise disappear.

10. Stakeholder and governance analysis

Model governance should sit with the communities that understand meaning, while publication and conformance can be coordinated centrally.

Clinicians, social-care professionals, analysts, terminologists, records specialists and software teams see different aspects of the same information. A model becomes fragile when any one group can define it without the others, or when supplier configuration is the only surviving expression of meaning.

Domain stewardship

Own clinical and operational meaning close to the profession and service that creates it.

Semantic coordination

Publish shared definitions, value sets, model identifiers and mappings through governed registries and terminology services.

Technical conformance

Validate profiles, templates and schemas mechanically, but do not mistake structural validation for complete semantic assurance.

11. Coherence and consistency

Coherence comes from traceability across models, not from forcing all models to look alike.

A mature architecture should be able to trace a concept from its business definition through clinical content models, exchange profiles, persistence structures and analytical derivatives. That chain is more valuable than an enterprise model that claims to eliminate translation but merely hides it.

12. Clarity and drafting

Name the modelling level explicitly.

Terms such as “data model”, “canonical model”, “clinical model” and “logical model” are routinely used without specifying their abstraction or purpose. Requirements should say whether they mean a concept system, information requirement, logical content model, exchange profile, persistence model or analytical common data model.

Weak statement

“FHIR will be the national data model.”

Problem: does not say whether FHIR is the exchange contract, persistence model, clinical content model or analytical representation.

Stronger statement

“National clinical information requirements will be governed independently and mapped to FHIR profiles for specified exchange use cases.”

Preservation statement

“Every transformation shall retain traceability to the source model, model version, terminology version and mapping used at the time.”

14. Recommended actions

Make information-model governance a first-class architecture capability.

1. Establish a model taxonomy

Require every model artefact to state its purpose, abstraction level, owner, version, lifecycle status and intended consumers.

2. Maintain source-to-target mappings

Record mappings centrally with semantic-loss notes rather than burying them in interface engines, ETL code or supplier configuration.

3. Separate requirements from implementation profiles

Keep clinically agreed information requirements durable even when FHIR, CDA, openEHR or another implementation technology changes.

4. Preserve historical model versions

Longitudinal data cannot be interpreted safely if the definitions and terminology versions that governed its creation are discarded.

5. Govern canonical models narrowly

Where canonical mediation is useful, define its bounded scope and do not allow it to become the unexamined enterprise source of semantic truth.

6. Test semantic round-trips

For critical transformations, test whether information can be reconstructed with clinically important distinctions intact, not merely whether schemas validate.

16. Limitations

What this navigator does not claim.

It is a professional architecture guide rather than a formal conformance specification. Individual standards contain substantially more detail than can be represented here. Model adoption varies by jurisdiction, specialty and vendor. CDISC SDTM v3.0 is still shown as resolving public-review comments on the current CDISC site, and ISO 13606-1 Edition 3 remains under development, so those items are explicitly treated as evolving rather than settled.
