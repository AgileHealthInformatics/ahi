Orientation

Start with the problem, not the standard

Healthcare standards are difficult to navigate because terminology, information content, exchange, persistence, analytics, assurance and professional competence are often discussed as though they were alternatives. They are not. A useful architecture normally combines several standards, each with a distinct role.

Taxonomy

First identify what kind of standard you need

A standards catalogue becomes more useful when each artefact is understood by the job it performs.

Process / knowledge standard
Describes workflow, decisions, cases or executable clinical knowledge.
Terminology / classification
Defines meaning or categorisation. It does not define the whole record or interface.
Content / information standard
Defines what information should be present and structured for a purpose.
Exchange / integration specification
Defines how information is technically represented, constrained and transported.
Persistence / analytical model
Structures information for longitudinal storage or secondary analysis.
Assurance / management standard
Defines governance, risk, safety, privacy, cyber or product lifecycle expectations.
Professional standard
Defines competence, conduct, responsibility or professional registration.
Access-control / identity standard
Establishes identity, authentication, authorization, policy or enforcement patterns. These are related but distinct concerns.

Interpretation

How to read the scores

Current use estimates practical penetration or maturity in relevant UK health and care settings. A score of 5 means pervasive, mandatory or deeply embedded within the applicable domain, not flawless use everywhere. Potential estimates strategic usefulness over the next several years if implemented competently. The measures are deliberately independent.

Jurisdiction

Jurisdictional interpretation

International standards can be technically relevant across many health systems, but national content standards, implementation guides, terminology editions, legal rules and assurance requirements differ. Use the jurisdiction filter to identify the local overlay rather than assuming a globally applicable standard has identical status everywhere.

England

The NHS Standards Directory provides the most explicit public catalogue of national information standards and assurance requirements among the four nations. SNOMED CT, relevant classifications, UK Core governance, DSPT and the DCB clinical-safety standards have formal status within defined scopes. DTAC adds a national product-assessment baseline. Professionals should always inspect the current standard page and Information Standards Notice rather than relying on the name alone.

Scotland

Scotland’s health and social care data strategy identifies the importance of common information standards and specifically cites SNOMED CT, ICD-10, OPCS, DICOM, FHIR and openEHR within the interoperability landscape. Its 2025 update reported that the Data Standards Sub-board was developing and piloting an assurance process for preferred standards. English mandates should not be assumed to apply.

Wales

Digital Health and Care Wales maintains national information standards, classifications, reference data and terminology services. NHS Wales has adopted FHIR R4 as a foundation through a Welsh Health Circular, contributes to UK Core and develops Welsh profiles where needed. The Welsh Reference Data and Terminology Service supplies SNOMED CT and FHIR-compliant code systems, value sets and concept maps.

Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland should be treated as a distinct HSC governance environment. Published strategy emphasises standardised pathways, system integration, cyber security and data-enabled decision-making, but a detailed public standards catalogue comparable to England’s is less visible. International and UK-wide standards remain relevant, but HSCNI and Department of Health requirements should be verified at procurement and design.

Europe / European Union

The European standards context is increasingly shaped by the European Health Data Space (EHDS). The Regulation entered into force in March 2025 and is being implemented progressively. For primary use, the first priority categories, patient summaries and ePrescriptions/eDispensations, are due to apply from March 2029, with medical images, laboratory results and hospital discharge reports following from March 2031. The European Electronic Health Record Exchange Format (EEHRxF) becomes particularly important because harmonised EHR interoperability components will be expected to import and export priority health data using that format.

Europe should therefore be treated as a distinct jurisdictional option, not as another UK nation. International standards such as HL7, DICOM, IHE, ISO, openEHR, OMOP, CDISC, DDI, SDMX and CODATA may be technically relevant across Europe, but their legal or procurement status varies by Member State and by EHDS implementation. MyHealth@EU provides the operational cross-border infrastructure for services including patient summaries and ePrescriptions, while HealthData@EU addresses secondary use. National requirements remain relevant alongside the common European layer.

United States

The US overlay is unusually influential because federal policy links data-content standards, FHIR implementation guides, certification and nationwide exchange. USCDI v7 is the latest published national core dataset, while US Core 9.0.0 is the current published US Core release and aligns to USCDI v6 rather than v7. That version gap is important: 'latest USCDI' and 'latest US Core' are not automatically a matched pair. TEFCA adds a separate nationwide exchange-governance layer.

Canada

Canada combines pan-Canadian content and exchange standards with provincial and territorial implementation. CACDI Version 2, published in March 2026, defines the core data content, while CA Core+ provides the corresponding national FHIR profile foundation. CA Core+ remained under active development in 2026, so implementers need to distinguish a pan-Canadian direction from the exact version adopted by a province, territory or programme.

Australia

Australia now has a comparatively explicit national stack. AUCDI provides core data content, AU Core defines minimum FHIR profiles and RESTful interactions, and SNOMED CT-AU plus Australian Medicines Terminology provide national terminology. Australian Digital Health Agency guidance updated in August 2026 states that AU Core is required for FHIR connections to My Health Record and AUCDI is the reference for shared FHIR data where applicable, with exact versions defined by implementation guides and conformance profiles.

New Zealand

New Zealand has one of the clearest national policy positions in the navigator. Health NZ states that FHIR must be used for new health data exchange solutions and applies a FHIR-first policy to all new builds. NZ Base is the national FHIR foundation, NZCDI defines the core data content, and SNOMED CT is required for important clinical data. Health NZ also maintains HISO standards and the NZ Health Terminology Service.

Interpretive rule: A jurisdiction tag means that a standard or framework has material relevance there. It does not imply identical legal status, procurement status, implementation guidance or mandated version. International standards should be combined with the applicable national or regional overlay.

  1. Starting with a favoured standard rather than a defined problem. Specify the use case, lifecycle stage and decision boundary first.
  2. Assuming exchange equals persistence. A FHIR payload, an openEHR longitudinal repository and an OMOP analytical database are different architectural concerns.
  3. Assuming syntactic conformance creates semantic interoperability. Terminology, information content, units, metadata, provenance and contextual interpretation remain essential.
  4. Using a terminology as though it were the whole data standard. SNOMED CT represents clinical meaning but still requires structures, bindings and governance.
  5. Using a classification for point-of-care meaning. ICD and OPCS support aggregation and statistics; they do not replace detailed clinical terminology.
  6. Confusing assurance with certification or regulation. DTAC, DSPT, DCB0129/DCB0160, ISO certification and medical-device regulation answer different questions.
  7. Buying the supplier’s safety case. Deployment and operational safety responsibilities remain with the deploying organisation where the applicable framework says so.
  8. Assuming data that exchanges correctly is analysis-ready. Research and analytics require provenance, quality, transformation, modelling and appropriate governance.
  9. Ignoring professional competence. Standards do not implement themselves. The competence to interpret, profile, test, govern and assure them is part of the solution.
  10. Assuming CDS Hooks is the decision logic. CDS Hooks defines when and how decision support is invoked and returned. The clinical knowledge, evidence, rules, terminology, safety case and governance still need to be specified separately.
  11. Confusing authentication with authorization. Proving identity does not establish that the user or application is entitled to access a particular patient, record, function or data element.
  12. Treating an access token as the access policy. OAuth, SMART and IUA can convey authorization context, but the resource server still needs explicit, governed policy and enforcement logic.
  13. Ignoring emergency, consent and purpose-of-use rules. Healthcare access control often needs context beyond role membership, including relationships, care context, patient preferences, legal basis and break-glass procedures.
  14. Treating an EHR functional model as though it were an implementation architecture. HL7 EHR-S FM describes functions and conformance criteria; ISO 18308 describes architectural requirements; ISO 13606 and openEHR address information architecture in different ways; FHIR primarily addresses exchange. They occupy different layers of the problem.
  15. Assuming a national core dataset is the same thing as a national FHIR profile. USCDI/US Core, CACDI/CA Core+, AUCDI/AU Core and NZCDI/NZ Base are complementary pairs with different responsibilities.
  16. Assuming FHIR replaces HL7 V2 everywhere. HL7 V2 remains deeply embedded in operational event-driven integration. FHIR often complements it.
  17. Treating CDA and XDS as the same standard. CDA defines clinical document structure and semantics. XDS.b defines the infrastructure for registering, discovering and retrieving documents across enterprises.
  18. Treating HL7 V3 as simply an obsolete messaging format. V3 messaging is largely a legacy concern, but the RIM remains conceptually important because CDA and other artefacts were built from the V3 modelling framework.
  19. Assuming XDS-I replaces DICOM. XDS-I.b supports cross-enterprise imaging sharing; DICOM remains the core standard for imaging objects and services.
Selection method

A practical method for choosing standards

The method deliberately separates semantics, exchange, persistence, assurance and professional accountability so that one standard is not forced to solve the entire information lifecycle.

  1. Define the use case and success criteria. Separate direct care, workflow, secondary use, assurance and professional-capability needs.
  2. Identify jurisdiction and mandatory requirements. Determine national information standards, safety, cyber, privacy, regulatory and contractual obligations before choosing technology.
  3. Choose semantics and information content. Define terminology, content, classifications, units, identifiers and metadata.
  4. Choose representation and transport. Select FHIR, DICOM, IHE or other technical specifications only after the information requirements are understood.
  5. Choose persistence and analytical models separately. Do not force a single canonical representation across the whole data lifecycle.
  6. Add assurance according to risk. Include clinical safety, cyber security, privacy, AI governance and medical-device lifecycle requirements where relevant.
  7. Specify professional accountability. Identify the competence, authority and professional standards expected of those making and assuring the decisions.
  8. Profile, test and govern. Name versions and implementation guides, test conformance and real data fitness, and manage change throughout the lifecycle.
Scope and maintenance

A curated, living professional resource

This is a curated international navigator rather than an exhaustive inventory. It combines globally relevant standards with jurisdiction-specific overlays for the UK, European Union, United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. Inclusion is based on whether a standard or framework represents a distinct and practically important part of the health informatics landscape.

A six-month formal review cycle is appropriate, with event-driven updates for national standards notices, major version changes and regulatory developments. Particularly volatile areas in 2026 include FHIR implementation guides, clinical-safety standards, AI assurance, professional registration expectations and future classification transitions.

Research and secondary-use coverage now includes cross-domain FAIR interoperability, research-data documentation and provenance, longitudinal research metadata, and statistical data and metadata exchange. These areas should be reviewed alongside changes to CODATA CDIF, the DDI product suite and SDMx.

Evidence note:Current-use scores are editorial estimates, not measurements from a census. In the interactive catalogue the estimate changes with the selected jurisdiction. Where jurisdiction-specific evidence is incomplete, the navigator uses cautious editorial judgement and retains the stated confidence level rather than presenting the score as measured adoption.
